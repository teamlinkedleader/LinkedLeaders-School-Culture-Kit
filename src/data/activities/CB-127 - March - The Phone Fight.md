# Culture Builder: The Phone Fight (March)

Stop losing the same argument daily and decide what you are actually protecting.

## Objective

Get your staff to one phone rule that everybody enforces identically. Most schools have a policy on paper and about fifteen in practice, which is worse than having none: students learn the rule is really about which adult is in front of them, staff who enforce it get treated as unreasonable, and everybody spends daily energy on an argument nobody wins. This is not about the right answer on phones. It is about ending the inconsistency.

## Time Needed

25 minutes in a staff meeting.

## Supplies / Setup

- Your written policy, whatever it currently says.
- An honest account of what actually happens in five different classrooms.
- A couple of students, if you are brave enough, though this can be done without them.
- Chart paper.

## How It Works

1. **Establish the gap out loud (5 minutes).** Ask the room to say what the rule is. You will get several answers. Then ask how many enforce it every time, and watch the room. Naming the gap is the entire foundation of the session, because everyone has been quietly pretending it is not there.
2. **Ask what you are actually protecting (7 minutes).** This is the question that makes the rest solvable. Attention in lessons? Social connection at break? Safeguarding, filming and photographs? Sleep and homework? These want different rules, and most policies fail because they try to do all four with one sentence and end up doing none.
3. **Write the rule for the thing you most want to protect (6 minutes).** One rule, one sentence, one place. "Phones in bags, not pockets, from the first bell to the last" is enforceable. "Appropriate use" is not, and unenforceable rules are what produced the inconsistency in the first place.
4. **Agree the response, not just the rule (5 minutes).** What happens the first time, and the second. If a member of staff has to invent the response in the moment, you are back to fifteen policies by Friday. Make the first response identical and boring.
5. **Say the reason to students, once, properly (2 minutes to plan).** Not "because it is the rule". The actual reason, in one sentence, said the same way by every adult. Students argue with arbitrary rules and largely comply with explained ones, and staff who can say the reason enforce more consistently because they believe it.

## Why It Works

- Inconsistency costs more than any policy setting. A rule enforced by half the adults teaches students that rules are about personalities, which then applies to every other rule you have.
- Naming what you are protecting is what makes a rule writable. Attention, social time, safeguarding and sleep are four different problems and one sentence cannot serve them all.
- Enforceability beats ambition. A modest rule everyone applies changes behaviour; an ideal rule half the staff ignore changes nothing and costs credibility.
- Agreeing the response prevents drift. Most inconsistency is not disagreement about the rule, it is fifteen different improvised reactions.
- Explaining the reason moves compliance from confrontation to agreement for most students, and gives staff something to say that is not "because I said so".

## Script for the Leader

Opening the meeting:

"I am going to ask you all what our phone rule is, and I already know we are going to get four different answers. That is not a criticism of anyone. It is the actual state of it, and it means the students have learned that the rule depends on which of us is standing there. Today we are not deciding who is right about phones. We are deciding one rule, one response, and one reason, and then all of us doing it the same way."

## Facilitator Tips

- Do not open with your own preferred answer. If staff think the outcome is decided, you get compliance in the room and none in the corridor.
- Be honest that a stricter rule everyone applies beats a looser one half apply, and also the reverse. The consistency is doing more work than the setting.
- Include the staff who currently do not enforce it and ask why. The answer is usually that they lost the argument once and it was not worth it, which is fixable.
- Agree the exceptions explicitly. Medical monitoring, a student with a specific plan, an emergency at home. Unstated exceptions become the crack the rule falls through.
- Tell families the rule and the reason at the same time as students, or you will have the argument twice.
- Review it once, six weeks later, and change it if it is not being applied. A rule you will not review is a rule you have decided not to enforce.
