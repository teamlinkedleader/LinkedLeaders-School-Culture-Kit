# Culture Builder: Recognition Beyond Grades (April)

Audit every award your school gives and find out which students can never win one.

## Objective

List every award, certificate and honour your school hands out in a year, then work out which students are structurally incapable of receiving any of them. In most schools a large group of children is ineligible for every single award from the day they enrol, because everything on the list rewards achievement or perfect attendance, and both are heavily determined by circumstances the student does not control.

## Time Needed

25 minutes with a small group, in April, before end-of-year awards get planned.

## Supplies / Setup

- Every award your school gives: honour roll, attendance, citizenship, subject awards, sports, whatever the PTO funds.
- Last year's recipient list if you have it.
- Two or three staff who know the students least likely to be on it, including a paraprofessional if you can.
- The willingness to retire an award somebody is fond of.

## How It Works

1. **List every award and what it actually measures (7 minutes).** Be precise. Honour roll measures grades. Perfect attendance measures health, housing stability, and whether an adult can drive them. Citizenship usually measures compliance and being liked by adults. Write the real measure next to each one, not the stated one.
2. **Pick three real students and check their eligibility (6 minutes).** Not hypothetical students. Three actual children: one with a chronic health condition, one whose family moves a lot, one who is doing well but quietly. Go down the list and mark what each could ever win. For some of them the answer is nothing, in any year, no matter what they do.
3. **Retire or fix what cannot be earned (5 minutes).** Perfect attendance is the clearest case. It rewards luck and punishes asthma, and it also encourages sick children into school. Either retire it or change it to something earnable, like most-improved attendance.
4. **Add two awards a different kind of student can win (5 minutes).** Effort over a term. Doing something hard badly and continuing. Being the person others go to. Fixing something nobody asked you to fix. The requirement is that a student in the bottom quartile academically can genuinely win it without it being a consolation prize.
5. **Check who actually won last year (2 minutes).** If the same twelve students hold most of the awards, your recognition system is measuring one thing repeatedly rather than recognising many things.

## Why It Works

- A student who works out in third grade that no award in the building is available to them has learned something about school that no amount of encouragement will undo.
- Naming what each award really measures is the whole audit. Almost every school has at least one award that measures household circumstances and calls it character.
- Perfect attendance is the most common and most defensible-sounding example, and it fails on two counts at once: it is unearnable for some children and it actively encourages sick kids into the building.
- Awards a struggling student can genuinely win only work if they are real. Students detect a consolation prize instantly and it does more damage than being left out.
- Checking last year's recipients exposes concentration. Recognition spread across twelve students is not recognition, it is a ranking with certificates.

## Script for the Leader

For the staff conversation:

"We give out fourteen awards. I went through them with three real kids in mind, and for one of them the answer is that she cannot win any of them, in any year, no matter what she does, because she has a condition that keeps her home and she is a C student. That is not a gap, that is a message, and she has already received it. So perfect attendance is going, and we are adding two things she could actually earn. Not consolation prizes. Real ones, that a kid at the bottom of the class can win outright."

## Facilitator Tips

- Use real students, not categories. "Students facing barriers" produces sympathy; a named child produces changes.
- Expect resistance on perfect attendance, usually from adults who value it rather than from families.
- Do not create an award for effort that is only ever given to struggling students. Kids read that immediately, and so do their peers.
- Include a paraprofessional or a lunch supervisor in the conversation. They see a completely different set of student strengths than teachers do.
- Check that the new awards are announced with the same weight as the academic ones. An award given quietly at the end is a rank, not a recognition.
- Ask the students themselves what should be recognised that is not. Their answers are consistently better than the adult version.
