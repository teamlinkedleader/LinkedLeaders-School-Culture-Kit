# Culture Builder: Mid-Year in Plain English (January)

Tell families how their kid is actually doing, in language a human being uses.

## Objective

Send every family a short, plain-language note about their child at the midpoint, separate from the report card. Report cards are compliance documents written in institutional language, and a large share of your families cannot decode them or do not read them. This is three sentences per student, in ordinary English, saying what is going well, what is hard, and what would help.

## Time Needed

25 minutes per staff member, given inside a staff meeting or a protected block.

## Supplies / Setup

- The class list, and whatever each teacher already knows. This should not require new assessment.
- A three-line frame on the board so nobody has to invent a format.
- A translation plan, sorted before the block rather than during it.
- Your own version, written about the school as a whole, sent the same week.

## How It Works

1. **Send yours first, about the whole school (leader, before the block).** A short plain note to all families: here is what is going well at this school halfway through, here is what we are finding hard, here is what we are doing about it. Modelling the honesty is what stops the staff notes from being uniformly positive mush.
2. **Give the three-line frame (5 minutes of the block).** One: something specific this child is doing well, not a grade. Two: one thing that is hard for them right now, stated without euphemism or blame. Three: one concrete thing that would help, that a family could actually do. That is the whole note.
3. **Ban the code (3 minutes).** Put the banned phrases on the board. "Working below grade level." "Struggles with focus." "Not meeting standards." "Needs to apply himself." These are institutional phrases that either mean nothing to a family or land as an accusation. Say the real thing: "He can read the words but loses the thread by the end of the page."
4. **Write (12 minutes).** Three sentences per student. It moves faster than people expect once the frame is fixed. Teachers with 150 students write for one class this round and rotate next time rather than doing a shallow version for everyone.
5. **Send, translated, and say a reply is welcome but not required (5 minutes).** Explicitly: you do not have to write back. Many families experience any school communication as a demand, and saying so removes the dread.

## Why It Works

- The report card is a legal record, not a communication. Families with lower literacy, English as a second language, or bad school experiences of their own frequently cannot use it, and those are often the families whose children most need the partnership.
- Naming one hard thing plainly, in January, is what makes the note credible and what makes it actionable. A note that is entirely positive is pleasant and useless.
- Attaching one concrete, doable suggestion converts information into something a family can act on. "Read more with him" is not concrete. "Ask him to tell you what happened in the chapter, not to read it out loud" is.
- Doing it separately from the report card matters. Attached to the report card it becomes part of the compliance document and is read the same way, which is to say often not at all.
- Saying no reply is needed materially raises the number of families who reply.

## Script for the Leader

For the note home about the school:

"Halfway through the year, here's an honest picture. Attendance is better than last year and the eighth grade in particular has turned a corner. What's hard: we've had three long-term substitute placements this year and that has been disruptive, and I know some of you have felt it. What we're doing: two of those roles are now permanently filled starting this month. Your child's teacher is sending a short note this week about your own kid specifically. You don't need to write back to either of us, though we're always glad when you do."

## Facilitator Tips

- Sort translation before the block. A note that arrives in a language a family cannot read communicates something worse than no note.
- Protect the time. Assigned as homework, this becomes three generic sentences written at 10pm and families can tell.
- Hold the line on the banned phrases. Institutional language is a habit, not a choice, and it will creep back in by student twelve.
- For a secondary teacher with 150 students, one class per round on a rotation beats a hollow version for all of them.
- Read a random ten before they go out. Not to approve them, but because you will learn a great deal about how your staff talk about children.
- Expect a handful of replies from families you have never heard from. Those are the ones to follow up on personally.
