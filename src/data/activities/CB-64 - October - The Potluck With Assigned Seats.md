# Culture Builder: The Potluck With Assigned Seats (October)

A staff lunch where the seating chart does the work nobody else will.

## Objective

Run the October staff potluck you were probably going to run anyway, and add the one design change that makes it matter: assigned seats that deliberately mix people who never talk. Left alone, a staff lunch reproduces the exact social map that already exists. Third grade sits with third grade, the office eats in the office, and the custodian eats standing up. Twenty-five minutes of seating design fixes that for free.

## Time Needed

25 minutes of lunch, plus about 15 minutes of setup the day before.

## Supplies / Setup

- Your existing sign-up sheet for food. Keep it simple and keep it genuinely optional, because a potluck that pressures people who cannot afford to contribute is worse than no potluck.
- Table numbers and a name card for every adult.
- A seating chart you build yourself, deliberately.
- One conversation prompt per table, printed and on the table.
- Coverage arranged so classified staff can actually attend. This is the step schools skip and it is the step that decides whether this is a staff lunch or a teacher lunch.

## How It Works

1. **Solve coverage first (10 minutes of setup).** Before anything else, figure out how the custodian, the office staff, the food service team, and the paraprofessionals get 25 minutes free at the same time. If they cannot come, cancel and reschedule. A staff event that structurally excludes the classified team says something louder than the event does.
2. **Build the chart to break the map (5 minutes of setup).** Every table gets a mix: someone from the office, someone classified, a veteran, a first-year, and people from at least three different grade levels or departments. Nobody sits with their team. Put your own name at a table where you know the fewest people.
3. **Put a prompt on every table (during).** One question, printed, no facilitation. Good ones: what job did you have before this one, what is something you are good at that nobody here knows about, who was the teacher that made you want to do this. Keep it personal and keep it off school operations.
4. **Sit down and stay down (during).** Do not float, do not work the room, do not clear plates. A leader circulating at a staff lunch is a leader hosting an event. A leader eating a full lunch at one table with a name card is a member of the staff. Pick the second one.
5. **Capture one thing per table (2 minutes at the end).** Each table writes on a card the most surprising thing they learned about someone at that table, no names required. Post the cards. It takes two minutes and it is the only reason anyone remembers this lunch in November.

## Why It Works

- Unassigned seating guarantees the people who already know each other spend an hour together, which is pleasant and changes nothing.
- Mixing grade levels and job types is where the real value sits. Most staff conflict in a school is between groups who have never had a personal conversation, and one lunch does not fix that but it is the only thing that starts it.
- Solving coverage for classified staff first is the piece that makes this credible. Everyone notices who is in the room and everyone notices who is not.
- A printed prompt removes the awkwardness that kills mixed-table conversation in the first four minutes.
- The leader sitting down for the whole lunch is the entire signal. Staff read a floating principal as a supervisor and a seated one as a colleague.

## Script for the Leader

For the announcement:

"Potluck Thursday, and yes, there are assigned seats. I know. Here's why: every time we do this, we sit with the same people we already eat with, and we leave knowing exactly what we walked in knowing. So I made a chart. You're sitting with people you don't work next to. There's a question on every table. Bring food if you want to and don't if you can't, nobody's tracking it. Coverage is handled, which means everybody comes, including the office and the crew."

## Facilitator Tips

- Never make food a requirement or track who brought what. The moment it becomes an obligation it costs money that some of your staff do not have.
- Expect grumbling about assigned seats and do it anyway. The grumbling ends about six minutes in, every time.
- Twenty-five minutes is the right length. An hour turns it into an event people have to manage around.
- Do not use the time for announcements. Not one. The second an agenda appears, this becomes a meeting with food.
- Run it again in February with a different chart. October's version is easy because everyone is still fresh; February's is the one people need.

## Display Idea

The table cards go up in the staff room under a header like "Things We Didn't Know." A wall of small surprises about colleagues is a genuinely good thing to walk past in November.
