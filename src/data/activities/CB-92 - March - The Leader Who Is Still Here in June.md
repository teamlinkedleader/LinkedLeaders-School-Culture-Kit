# Culture Builder: The Leader Who Is Still Here in June (March)

Track one honest week of your own, then change two structural things so you make it to June.

## Objective

Spend one week recording what your job actually costs you, then change two things about how it runs. March is when school leaders quietly decide they cannot do another year, and the decision is usually made in a state of depletion nobody around them can see. Every other activity in this collection is about the people you lead. This one is about whether you are still leading them next year.

## Time Needed

Five minutes a day for one week, then 25 minutes to act on it.

## Supplies / Setup

- A card in your pocket or a note on your phone. Five entries a day, no system.
- Your calendar for the week, which you will compare against reality.
- One person outside the building you trust. A mentor, a peer principal, a coach, a friend who is not your spouse.
- Enough honesty to write down the unflattering version.

## How It Works

1. **Track five things a day for one week (5 minutes a day).** What time you arrived. What time you left. Whether you ate lunch sitting down. How many times you were interrupted during something that required thought. And one word for how you felt at the end of the day. Do not improve your behaviour because you are tracking it; the point is the baseline.
2. **Read the week as if it belonged to somebody else (5 minutes).** If a teacher you supervised handed you this week, what would you say to them? Most leaders discover they would be genuinely concerned about a staff member living this week and are entirely untroubled by living it themselves.
3. **Separate what only you can do from what you have absorbed (7 minutes).** Two columns. The first is genuinely yours: the hard conversations, the decisions, the things where your presence is the point. The second is everything you have picked up because it was faster than delegating. The second column is usually larger.
4. **Make two structural changes, not two intentions (8 minutes).** Structural means it holds when you are tired. A blocked hour that nothing may take. A standing handoff of one recurring duty to a named person. A rule that you leave at five on Wednesdays with the phone off. "Take better care of myself" is not a change and will not survive April.
5. **Tell one person outside the building what you changed (5 minutes).** Someone with no stake in your school who will ask you about it in a month. This is the entire enforcement mechanism, and without it the two changes last about nine days.

## Why It Works

- School leaders leave the profession disproportionately, and the exit decision is typically made in late winter, in private, by someone whose depletion is invisible to their staff.
- Tracking beats reflecting. A leader asked how their week went will report a mood; a leader with five data points a day for a week has evidence, and evidence is harder to talk yourself out of.
- The transfer test in step two works because leaders hold themselves to standards they would never impose on their staff, and seeing it as somebody else's week breaks that.
- The absorbed-work column is where the recoverable time is. Almost none of it was delegated away, and most of it was taken on in a hurry because doing it yourself was faster that day.
- Structural changes survive bad weeks; intentions do not. And the external person is what makes it real, because everyone inside your building has an interest in you being endlessly available.

## Script for the Leader

There is no audience for this one. The version you say to the person outside the building:

"I tracked a week in March. I averaged eleven and a half hours, ate lunch sitting down once, and the word I wrote down four days out of five was 'flat.' If one of my teachers handed me that week I'd have pulled them into my office. So: Thursday mornings are blocked for classrooms and nothing takes that block, and Rob has taken over the entire duty roster permanently, not just when I ask. I need you to ask me in a month whether both of those are still true."

## Facilitator Tips

- Track a normal week, not a quiet one. A week chosen because it looks manageable produces useless data.
- Do not clean up the numbers before you look at them. The unflattering version is the one worth acting on.
- Two changes. A leader who announces five in March has announced none, and will be running all five badly by April.
- Pick the outside person carefully. Someone inside the building, and often a spouse, has too much stake in the answer to push you.
- Do not share the tracked week with your staff. This is not a bid for sympathy and it will read as one.
- Re-run it in October. If the two changes from March are gone by then, that is the finding, and it is worth more than the original data.
- This is the activity most likely to be skipped, by the person least able to afford skipping it.
