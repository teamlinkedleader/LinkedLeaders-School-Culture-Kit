# Culture Builder: Design Thinking Sprint (Bonus)

Build a rough solution to one real problem in ninety minutes, then test it on the people it affects.

## Objective

Take one specific, bounded school problem through five stages in a single session: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test. The discipline that makes it work is that you leave with something rough and tested rather than a plan to plan. Most school improvement conversations stop at Ideate, which is why they produce lists nobody implements.

## Time Needed

90 minutes. Best as a faculty meeting block, a PD half-day, or a retreat session.

## Supplies / Setup

- One problem, chosen in advance and narrow. "Improve school culture" is not a problem; "the hallway between third and fourth period is chaos" is.
- Chart paper, markers, sticky notes, and whatever junk is around for prototyping.
- Access to the people affected. Six students, or six staff, available for the last 20 minutes. Without them the Test stage does not happen and the sprint is theatre.
- Small teams of four to five.

## How It Works

1. **Empathize, 20 minutes.** Go and ask the people who live with the problem what it is actually like. Interview students in the hallway, or the staff who cover the duty. Do not theorise from the room. This is the stage schools skip and it is the one that changes the answer.
2. **Define, 15 minutes.** Write the problem as one sentence from the perspective of the person affected. "Seventh graders need a way to get to fourth period without pushing, because the stairwell bottleneck makes them late and then they get in trouble for it." If the sentence contains a solution, rewrite it.
3. **Ideate, 20 minutes.** Volume before quality. Every team generates as many options as possible with no evaluation, then picks one to build. The no-evaluation rule matters, because experienced staff kill ideas early and the killed ones include the good ones.
4. **Prototype, 20 minutes.** Build something rough enough to react to. A drawn floor plan, a mocked-up schedule, a script, a signup sheet, a role-play. Rough is the requirement. A polished prototype gets defended instead of tested.
5. **Test, 15 minutes.** Put it in front of the actual people. Ask what would go wrong. Write down every objection without arguing. Then decide, out loud, what happens on Monday: adopt, revise, or abandon. All three are legitimate outcomes and saying so in advance is what makes the feedback honest.

## Why It Works

- Interviewing the affected people first is the entire difference between a sprint and a meeting. Schools consistently solve the problem they imagined rather than the one that exists.
- Writing the problem from the user's perspective prevents the most common failure, which is defining the problem as the absence of your preferred solution.
- Rough prototypes get honest feedback. Polished ones get politeness, because nobody wants to criticise something somebody clearly worked hard on.
- Testing in the same session is what stops it becoming a plan. A tested rough idea beats an untested good one.
- Naming abandon as an acceptable outcome removes the pressure that makes teams defend weak prototypes.

## Script for the Leader

"One problem, ninety minutes, and we are not leaving with a plan. We are leaving with something rough that we have already shown to the kids it affects. First twenty minutes, nobody theorises: go and ask them. Then we define it in their words, generate as many options as we can without shooting any of them down, and build something crude enough to be honest about. At the end, adopt, revise or bin it. Binning it is a real answer and I will not be disappointed."

## Facilitator Tips

- Choose a genuinely narrow problem. The sprint fails on scope more than on anything else.
- Arrange the interviewees before the session. Without them the Test stage evaporates and the whole thing becomes a brainstorm.
- Enforce no evaluation during Ideate, firmly. One experienced sceptic can close the stage in ninety seconds.
- Keep prototypes deliberately crude. Ban laptops for that stage if you have to.
- Whatever gets adopted, implement it within two weeks. A sprint whose output never ships is worse than no sprint, because it proves the exercise was decorative.
- Run it on a problem you are willing to have solved differently than you would have solved it.

## Display Idea

Keep the prototype, however crude, and post it next to the objections it collected. In a month, add a note on what actually happened. That sequence is the most useful thing a staff room wall can show.
