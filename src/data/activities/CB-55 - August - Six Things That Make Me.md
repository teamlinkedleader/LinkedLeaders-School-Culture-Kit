# Culture Builder: Six Things That Make Me (August)

Every person in the building, adults included, puts six small things about themselves on a door.

## Objective

Give every student a fast, low-risk way to be known for something specific in the first weeks of school, and make the adults do it too. Six boxes, six small true things, posted where people walk past them. It works because it asks for detail rather than depth. A kid who will not answer "tell us about yourself" will happily draw a hockey stick, a dog, and a bowl of pho.

## Time Needed

20 minutes of class time. About 10 minutes of leader prep.

## Supplies / Setup

- One sheet of paper per person, folded into six boxes. That is the entire supply list.
- Markers or colored pencils if you have them, pencils if you do not.
- Your own six-box card, already finished and posted on your office door before the block runs.
- Tape.

## How It Works

1. **Post yours first (leader, before the block).** Fill in your own six boxes and tape the card to your office door where families and students can see it. Make at least two of them a little bit surprising and at least one of them slightly embarrassing. A principal's card that says "reading, family, coffee, running, my school, my dog" teaches everyone to be boring.
2. **Show the rule (2 minutes).** Six boxes. Six things that are true and small. Not achievements, not favorites in the abstract. A specific thing: the exact food, the actual band, the sibling's name, the thing you are weirdly good at.
3. **Teacher goes first in the room (3 minutes).** The teacher fills theirs in on the document camera or the board while narrating, then answers one question about it. Adults modeling vulnerability at a very low stakes level is what unlocks the kids who would otherwise write nothing.
4. **Everyone makes theirs (10 minutes).** Words, drawings, or both. Nobody is graded, nobody is corrected, and a kid who wants to keep one box private draws a box with a lock in it. That is allowed and you should say so out loud.
5. **Post and walk (5 minutes).** Every card goes on the classroom door or the hallway outside it, adults' cards mixed in with students', not separated. Then the class takes three minutes to walk the hall and find one thing they have in common with someone they do not know.

## Why It Works

- Specific detail is what makes a person real to another person. "I like sports" produces nothing. "I play goalie and I'm scared of the puck" produces a friend by Thursday.
- Six small things is a low enough bar that the shy kid, the new kid, and the kid with almost no English can all finish it and have something on the wall like everybody else.
- Mixing adult cards in with student cards is the move most schools skip and the one that matters most. Kids find out their math teacher fishes, and the relationship changes for free.
- Posting it on doors puts it in traffic. A card in a folder is an assignment. A card on a door is a building that knows who is in it.
- The lock box option protects the kids with hard home situations, and offering it publicly tells every student the activity is safe.

## Script for the Leader

For introducing it to staff:

"Mine's already on my door, go read it before you judge the activity. Six small true things, that's it. The rule I care about is that you do yours in front of your kids and you put it on the door next to theirs. If the adult cards end up on a separate wall, we've built a bulletin board instead of a school where people know each other."

## Facilitator Tips

- Watch for the kid who writes six things about someone else, or six things that are clearly a performance. That is information, not misbehavior. Follow up privately.
- Do not theme it. "Six things about your summer" narrows it to kids who had a good summer.
- Secondary: run it in advisory and post outside the advisory room, or the sheer volume of cards makes the hallway unreadable.
- Keep the cards. February, Week 2 is a kindness activity that works far better when students already know six specific things about the person they draw.
- Refresh in January for mid-year arrivals. A new student in a hallway full of everyone else's cards, with no card of their own, feels exactly as bad as it sounds.

## Display Idea

Doors, not walls. Every classroom door, every office door, the nurse, the counselor, the custodian, the front desk. A visitor walking the building should be able to learn six real things about the first ten people they pass.
