# Culture Builder: The Hard Week Protocol (February)

Decide now what the building does when the week is genuinely bad, so you are not deciding it while it is.

## Objective

Write down, in advance, what your school does during a genuinely bad week. A student death, a staff crisis, a flu wave that takes out a third of the building, a community tragedy, or just the accumulated grind of late February. Decide what gets suspended, who is allowed to call it, and what nobody has to do. Every school improvises this and improvises it badly, because the people deciding are the people least able to think clearly at the time.

## Time Needed

25 minutes with your leadership team, in early February.

## Supplies / Setup

- Your leadership team, plus your counselor and your office manager.
- An honest memory of the last genuinely bad week you had, and what actually happened to people during it.
- The list of recurring obligations: meetings, deadlines, duties, observations, data entry, evening events.
- Somewhere public to post the result. This cannot live in a drawer.

## How It Works

1. **Recall the last bad week honestly (6 minutes).** What happened, and what did you keep doing anyway that you should have cancelled? Most teams find they ran a scheduled staff meeting or held an observation window during a week when nobody could think. Name it without blame.
2. **Decide what gets suspended automatically (7 minutes).** Write the list. Observations pause. Non-essential meetings cancel. Data deadlines move. Duty rosters get covered by admin. The word that matters is automatically: if it needs a decision in the moment, it will not happen.
3. **Decide who can call it, and make it more than one person (5 minutes).** If only the principal can declare a hard week, it will not be declared during the weeks when the principal is the one struggling. Name two or three people who can, including someone who is not an administrator.
4. **Write what nobody has to do (4 minutes).** Nobody has to be cheerful. Nobody has to attend the optional thing. Nobody has to explain why they need to go home. Nobody has to answer email after four. Stating these in advance is what lets people use them without asking permission, and asking permission is exactly what a person in a bad week cannot do.
5. **Post it and reference it before you need it (3 minutes).** Put it in the staff handbook and on the workroom wall. Mention it once in February when things are fine, so that when you invoke it in March nobody has to be told what it means.

## Why It Works

- Decisions made during a crisis are made by people with no capacity, and they default to carrying on as normal because carrying on requires no decision.
- Pre-authorising the suspension removes the guilt. A teacher who knows observations are automatically paused does not have to ask for grace, and asking is the barrier.
- Distributing who can call it is the part most schools get wrong. The leader is frequently the last to notice the building is underwater and sometimes the reason it is.
- Naming what nobody has to do is more useful than any support offer. The exhausting part of a hard week is the performance of being fine.
- Writing it in February is deliberate. Late February is the low point of the school year in most buildings, so the protocol gets written by people who can feel exactly why it is needed.

## Script for the Leader

"We're writing down what this building does in a genuinely bad week, and we're doing it now while nothing's wrong, because the last time we had one we were all too fried to think and we ran a staff meeting on the Tuesday after Marcus died. That was my call and it was the wrong one. Here's what suspends automatically next time, here's who can call it besides me, and here's the list of things nobody has to do. It goes on the workroom wall. I'd rather we never use it."

## Facilitator Tips

- Name a real past failure in step one, ideally one of yours. A protocol written from a hypothetical bad week is a policy document; one written from a real one is a plan.
- Make at least one non-administrator able to call it. A veteran teacher or your office manager will often see it before you do.
- Keep the list of suspensions short and absolute. Six things that definitely stop beat twenty things that might.
- Do not attach a wellness activity to it. A hard week does not need a yoga session; it needs less work.
- Say once, in a good week, that the protocol exists. An unmentioned protocol is not available to anyone when it matters.
- When you do invoke it, invoke it explicitly and by name. "We're in a hard week" is the sentence that gives everyone permission.

## Display Idea

A single posted page in the workroom titled with the date it was agreed and the names of the people who can call it. It should look ordinary and permanent, like a fire evacuation notice, because that is exactly what it is.
