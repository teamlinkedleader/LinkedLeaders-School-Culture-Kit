# Culture Builder: Staff Who Are Also Parents Here (April)

Ask the colleagues whose own children attend what it is actually like to be both.

## Objective

Sit down with the staff whose own children are students in your school and work out the norms nobody has ever written. They occupy two roles at once and get the benefits of neither: they cannot complain as a parent without it becoming a staffroom matter, they hear things about their own child's teacher in meetings, and their children are watched more closely than anyone else's. It is a common situation in schools and an almost universally unexamined one.

## Time Needed

20 minutes with that group. Off site, or at least out of the building, if you can.

## Supplies / Setup

- The list of staff whose children attend, which your office can produce in a minute.
- Nothing prepared. This is a listening meeting and an agenda would defeat it.
- A commitment to change at least one thing.

## How It Works

1. **Say the obvious thing first (3 minutes).** That they are in two roles, that the school has never acknowledged it, and that you want to know what it is actually like. Most of them will not have been asked in their entire career.
2. **Ask the three real questions (10 minutes).** What is hard about being both. What do you hear that you wish you did not. And what would you say as a parent if you did not work here. That last one produces the most valuable information in the meeting and you have to be prepared to sit still for it.
3. **Name the specific binds (4 minutes).** They usually cluster: hearing a colleague discuss their child's class, being unable to raise a concern about their child's teacher, their child being disciplined more visibly or less visibly than others, and being expected to be a staff member at parents' evening.
4. **Agree a small number of norms with them (3 minutes).** Who they raise a parent concern with, so it is not their own line manager. That their child gets the same treatment as any other, said out loud so their child's teacher knows too. That staffroom conversation about specific children stops being acceptable generally, which is a rule the whole school benefits from.
5. **Tell the rest of the staff what changed and why.** Without naming anyone's family situation. The norm about not discussing individual children in the staffroom is worth stating school-wide on its own merits.

## Why It Works

- Being an employee and a parent in the same building is a genuine bind, and almost no school has ever named it, so the people in it assume they are the only one finding it awkward.
- The question about what they would say if they did not work here is the most honest customer feedback available to a school leader, and it is sitting in the staffroom.
- Their children are genuinely treated differently, in both directions, and saying so out loud is what allows it to be corrected.
- Giving them a route to raise a parent concern that is not their own line manager solves a real conflict of interest that otherwise silences them.
- The staffroom norm about individual children improves the building for every family, not just this group.

## Script for the Leader

Opening:

"There are six of you who work here and have your own children in this school. As far as I know nobody has ever sat down with you about what that is actually like. You get the awkward bits of both roles and the protection of neither. I want to hear the hard version, including the thing you would say to me as a parent if you did not work for me. I will not hold that against you, and if I do, tell Chad."

## Facilitator Tips

- Hold it away from the staffroom. The conversation cannot happen in the room the conversation is about.
- Do not do this one to one. The group discovering that they all find it awkward is half the value.
- Expect at least one piece of genuinely uncomfortable feedback about the school. That is the point of asking and flinching will close the group permanently.
- Give them a named alternative for parent concerns, and make sure that person knows.
- Check how their children are actually treated. Staff children are frequently either shielded or made an example of, and both are visible to other students.
- Repeat annually. The group changes as children move through the school.
