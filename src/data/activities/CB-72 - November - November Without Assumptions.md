# Culture Builder: November Without Assumptions (November)

Audit the month for everything it quietly assumes about a kid's home, and fix the worst three.

## Objective

Go through everything your school is doing in November and find the places where it assumes a student has a family, a home, a table, food, and a reason to be grateful. Then fix the three worst. November is the month schools accidentally tell some of their kids that the version of life the building is celebrating is not theirs. This is 25 minutes of honest reading and three concrete changes.

## Time Needed

25 minutes, alone or with two trusted staff, in late October.

## Supplies / Setup

- Everything November: the calendar, the assemblies, the newsletter drafts, the announcement scripts, the classroom activities teachers have shared, the food drive materials, the art on the walls.
- Your enrollment data on housing instability, foster placement, and free and reduced lunch. The real numbers, not your impression of them.
- Your counselor or social worker, if you have one. They know which kids this lands on.
- A pen and a willingness to find things you wrote yourself.

## How It Works

1. **Read the month as one specific kid (10 minutes).** Pick a real student, unnamed to anyone else, who is in a shelter, a placement, or a house with nothing in it. Go through November item by item and ask what each one feels like to that kid. The "what are you thankful for" assembly. The food drive that ranks classes by how much they donate. The family recipe project. The break itself.
2. **Mark the three worst, not all of them (5 minutes).** You cannot rebuild November. Find the three items with the highest chance of doing real harm to a real kid and mark those. Perfectionism here produces a long list and zero changes.
3. **Fix them concretely (7 minutes).** Not "be sensitive." Change the thing. The food drive stops being a competition and stops announcing which class won. The gratitude prompt changes from "what are you thankful for at home" to "who is someone that has been good to you." The family recipe becomes a food you like and who you would want to share it with. The break letter home stops assuming the break is a good thing and includes what is open and where meals are.
4. **Tell staff what changed and why, in one paragraph (2 minutes).** Do not lecture and do not name any student. Name the change and the reason. Staff comply with rules and commit to reasons.
5. **Handle the break itself (1 minute to decide).** Decide what your building does about the five days off: who checks on which kids, what food goes home, what a family can call if something goes wrong. Assign it to a name before you leave for the week.

## Why It Works

- November's assumptions are invisible to the adults writing them, because most of those adults have a family, a table, and food, and are not thinking about the ones who do not.
- Reading the month as one specific student is what makes it concrete. Thinking about "our vulnerable population" produces sympathy. Thinking about one named kid in a shelter produces changes.
- Three fixes get made. Twenty get discussed and then November arrives unchanged.
- The competitive food drive is the single most common example, and it is the one that most reliably humiliates the families receiving the food while celebrating the families giving it.
- The five-day break is where the actual risk sits. For some students, school is the most stable thing they have, and a week without it is a week without meals, adults, and structure.

## Script for the Leader

The paragraph to staff:

"Three changes to November. The food drive isn't a competition this year and we're not announcing totals by class. The gratitude prompt is 'who has been good to you' instead of 'what are you thankful for at home.' And the letter going home before break includes what's open and who to call, because for some of our families a week off isn't a break. None of this is about anybody doing anything wrong. It's about noticing what we've been assuming."

## Facilitator Tips

- Do this in late October. Doing it in November means the materials are already out.
- Do not name the student you read the month through. Not to staff, not to your leadership team.
- Include your counselor or social worker in the read, and believe them when they flag something you think is fine.
- Watch for the tradition that is beloved and also harmful. Those are the hard ones and they are usually worth changing anyway, with a year of warning rather than a surprise.
- Keep the marked-up calendar. Next October it saves you twenty of these twenty-five minutes.
- Apply the same read to December, which has all the same assumptions and more of them.
