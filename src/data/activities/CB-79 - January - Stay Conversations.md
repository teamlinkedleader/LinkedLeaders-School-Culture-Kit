# Culture Builder: Stay Conversations (January)

Ask why they stay and what would make them leave, while they are still here to answer.

## Objective

Sit down one-to-one with staff and ask two questions: what makes you stay here, and what would make you leave. Twenty minutes each, no evaluation attached, and start in January because that is when people decide about next year and months before they tell you. An exit interview is a post-mortem. This is the same information while it is still worth something.

## Time Needed

20 minutes per person. Start in January and work through the staff over several weeks.

## Supplies / Setup

- A list of every staff member and a plan for the order. Start with the people you would be most damaged by losing, not the people who are easiest to talk to.
- A room that is not your office if your office is where hard conversations happen.
- Paper, not a laptop.
- Nothing else. No form, no rubric, no agenda document.

## How It Works

1. **Say what it is and what it is not, in the invitation (2 minutes of setup).** Tell people in advance: twenty minutes, not an evaluation, nothing goes in a file, and the questions are these two. Sending the questions ahead is what gets you a real answer instead of an improvised polite one.
2. **Ask what makes them stay, and shut up (7 minutes).** The first answer is usually "the kids" or "the team." That is the warm-up, not the answer. Ask "what else" once and then wait through the silence. The second and third answers are the ones you can actually act on.
3. **Ask what would make them leave (7 minutes).** Then take it seriously, however it lands. If the honest answer is your leadership, a specific colleague, the commute, money, or a schedule, write it down without defending anything. The instinct to explain is the instinct that ends the conversation and guarantees you never hear the truth again.
4. **Ask the third question if there is time (2 minutes).** "What is one thing I could change that would make next year better for you?" Small and specific. This is where you get the fixable stuff, and fixable stuff is the point.
5. **Change one thing, visibly, and say why (2 minutes).** After the round, pick something you heard more than once and fix it publicly. Name that it came from these conversations without attributing it to anyone. That single act is what determines whether anybody tells you the truth next January.

## Why It Works

- Teachers decide about next year in late winter and resign in spring. January is early enough that what you learn can still change the outcome; April is not.
- The two questions surface completely different information. Why people stay tells you what to protect. What would make them leave tells you what to fix, and the second list is almost never what leaders assume it is.
- Asking someone what would make them leave, and not flinching, is itself the retention move. It signals that their leaving would be a loss rather than a personnel event.
- Writing on paper instead of a laptop changes the register of the conversation. A screen makes it an intake interview.
- Acting on one thing publicly is the entire mechanism. A round of listening that produces no visible change teaches your staff that you collect feelings, and the next round gets you nothing.

## Script for the Leader

For the invitation:

"I'd like twenty minutes with you sometime in the next few weeks. It's not an evaluation, nothing goes in a file, and I'm not building up to anything. Two questions, and I'm sending them now so you can think: what makes you stay here, and what would make you leave. I'd rather hear the honest version now than read it in a resignation letter in April."

## Facilitator Tips

- Send the questions in advance. Ambushing someone with "what would make you leave" produces a reassuring lie.
- Do not defend anything, and especially not yourself. If the answer is about you, say "thank you, tell me more" and write it down. You can think about it later.
- Start with the people whose departure would hurt most. If you run out of time, you will have spent it on the right people.
- Do not take notes on names when people mention colleagues. Write the pattern, not the person.
- Never let it drift into evaluation, and never reference something from a stay conversation in an evaluation. One instance of that and the practice is dead permanently.
- Do a shorter version in April with anyone whose answer worried you.
- If several people name the same fixable thing, that is not a coincidence and it will be in your exit interviews next year.
