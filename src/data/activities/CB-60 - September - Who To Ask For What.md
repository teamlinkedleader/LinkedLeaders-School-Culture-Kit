# Culture Builder: Who To Ask For What (September)

One page that tells every adult exactly who to go to, so nobody guesses and nobody gets skipped.

## Objective

Build, with your staff, a single page that answers the question every adult in your building asks twenty times a year: who do I go to for this? Unclear roles are not a chart problem, they are a culture problem. They produce the same three overworked people fielding everything, a handful of staff who never get asked for anything, and new hires who learn the system by embarrassing themselves. This fixes it in one meeting.

## Time Needed

25 minutes in a staff meeting, September.

## Supplies / Setup

- Chart paper or a shared doc on the screen.
- Your actual org chart, which you will mostly ignore. The point is the real map, not the formal one.
- A list of the things people actually ask about: a broken device, a kid you are worried about, a field trip, a sub, a parent complaint, a supply order, a schedule change, an IEP question, a maintenance issue, a student who has not eaten.
- Your own honest list of what you personally should and should not be the first stop for.

## How It Works

1. **Say what you are not the answer to (5 minutes).** Go first and be specific. Name three things staff routinely bring to you that somebody else can solve faster and better, and name that person. A leader who claims everything ends up as the bottleneck for everything, and staff will not redirect themselves unless you do it first.
2. **List the real questions, not the categories (7 minutes).** Ask the room: what did you have to ask somebody about in the first three weeks? Write the actual questions in plain language. "Who do I tell when a kid says something scary" beats "student support referrals" every time, because the plain version is what people search for at 7:40 in the morning.
3. **Assign one name to each, and only one (8 minutes).** One name per line. Two names is the same as zero, because everyone assumes the other person has it. If a real question has no owner, that is the most valuable thing this meeting produces. Leave it blank and deal with it out loud.
4. **Find the people nobody was asked about (3 minutes).** Look at whose names appear zero times. Some of them are underused on purpose and some are underused because they are new, quiet, or classified staff. Ask what they know how to do that the page is missing, and add it.
5. **Post it in the places people actually stand (2 minutes).** The workroom, the copier, the front office, and taped inside a cabinet door in every classroom. Not emailed. An emailed one-pager is a document nobody can find in March.

## Why It Works

- Role clarity is a workload issue that reads as a respect issue. The three people who field everything are the three people closest to burning out, and they rarely say so.
- Written in the staff's own words, the page gets used. Written in administrative language, it gets ignored in favor of asking the nearest human.
- The blanks are the point. Every school has three or four real needs that no one owns, and they only surface when you try to write a single name next to them.
- Naming who you are not is the highest-leverage 5 minutes in the meeting. It gives staff explicit permission to route around you, which is the only way a leader stops being a bottleneck.
- Adding the invisible staff to the page changes how they are treated all year. Being on the who-to-ask sheet is a small, real form of belonging.

## Script for the Leader

"Three things people bring me constantly that I'm not the right answer for. Substitute problems, go to Dana, she'll solve it in four minutes and I'll take an hour. Tech that won't turn on, go to Marcus first, not me. And field trip paperwork, that's Angela, always was. I'm not pushing you away. I'm slowing you down and I want to stop. So we're going to write down who to actually ask for everything, in your words, and put one name next to each thing."

## Facilitator Tips

- One name per line. Hold this hard. The room will push for "either Dana or the office" and that is exactly the ambiguity you came to kill.
- If a line has no owner and you do not have one to give, say so plainly and put a date on when you will. Do not quietly assign it to yourself in the room.
- Include classified staff, and ask them directly what they get asked about that is not on the page. The office manager and the head custodian usually know more about how the building runs than the chart suggests.
- Hand the finished page to every new hire, and add it to the first-30-days card.
- Revisit it in January. Roles drift, people leave, and a stale who-to-ask sheet is worse than none because it sends people confidently to the wrong door.

## Display Idea

A single laminated page at the copier, the one place every adult in the building stands still for ninety seconds.
