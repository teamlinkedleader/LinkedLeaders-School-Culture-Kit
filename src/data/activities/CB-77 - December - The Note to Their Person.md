# Culture Builder: The Note to Their Person (December)

Write to the person who lives with your staff member and tell them what they did this year.

## Objective

Write to the household behind a staff member rather than to the staff member. A spouse, a partner, a parent, a grown child, whoever is on the other end of the late nights and the Sunday grading. Tell them, specifically, what their person did this year. The family of a school employee absorbs a real cost and hears almost nothing about the value. This is 25 minutes and a stamp, and it is the most disproportionately powerful thing on this list.

## Time Needed

25 minutes for four or five letters. Send them in the week before break.

## Supplies / Setup

- Notecards or letterhead and envelopes. Letterhead is worth it here; it makes the letter something a family keeps.
- Home addresses, handled with whatever approval your district requires.
- Stamps.
- A list of your staff and a note of who you know shares a household with someone. Where you do not know, "to the family of" works fine.

## How It Works

1. **Pick four or five and find the specific thing (10 minutes).** One real moment per person, the kind their family would never hear about. The night they stayed until eight to finish something for a kid who was struggling. The way they handled a parent who was unfair to them. The colleague they quietly carried through a hard month.
2. **Write to the household, not the employee (10 minutes).** Address it to the person or the family, and write about your staff member in the third person. "I wanted you to know what Elena did in October." Say what happened, say what it produced, and say that you know it cost their household something.
3. **Name the cost out loud (inside the letter).** One line acknowledging that the job takes evenings, weekends, and emotional bandwidth that belongs to them. This is the line that lands, and it is the line most leaders leave out because it feels like admitting something.
4. **Mail it to the house (3 minutes).** Not through the staff member. It has to arrive independently, addressed to someone else, or the whole effect collapses.
5. **Say nothing to the staff member (ongoing).** Do not mention it, do not ask if it arrived, do not follow up. They will tell you, usually awkwardly, sometimes weeks later. Let it be theirs.

## Why It Works

- The household of a school employee pays a real, uncompensated cost and receives essentially no acknowledgment from the institution collecting the benefit.
- Praise delivered to somebody else is a different order of thing than praise delivered to you. It cannot be brushed off as management technique, and the staff member does not have to perform modesty about it.
- Naming the cost is what makes it credible. A letter that only praises reads as a school extracting more goodwill. A letter that says "I know this takes evenings that belong to you" reads as honest.
- December is when families feel the job most, and it lands right when the tank is emptiest.
- It reaches the decision that keeps people in this profession. Teachers leave partly because their households absorb too much invisible cost. A family that feels seen is a family that supports staying.

## Script for the Leader

There is no script to deliver out loud. The letter itself:

"Dear Marta, I'm the principal at Lincoln, where James teaches sixth grade. I'm writing to you rather than to him because I don't think he'd tell you this himself. In October we had a student in real crisis, and James stayed late four nights running to build something that would work for that kid. It worked. That boy is still in school and doing well, and the honest reason is James. I know four late nights means four evenings that belonged to you and to your family, and I don't take that for granted. Thank you for letting us have him. I hope you both get a real rest over the break."

## Facilitator Tips

- Get whatever approval your district requires for using home addresses before you start, and ask the office rather than assuming.
- Where you do not know a household situation, address it to "the family of." Do not guess at a spouse's name or assume there is one.
- Be careful with anyone recently divorced, bereaved, or estranged. If you are not certain the letter lands somewhere warm, write to them directly instead.
- Four or five a year is right. This does not scale to the whole staff and it should not; a version of it sent to everyone becomes a form letter.
- Never mention it to the staff member first. The letter arriving unannounced is most of the effect.
- Handwrite the signature at minimum, and the whole thing if your handwriting is legible.
- Do not reuse phrasing between letters. Two families comparing notes and finding the same sentence undoes all of it.
