# Culture Builder: The Halfway Honest Check (January)

Grade yourself against the sentence you wrote in July, out loud, in front of your staff.

## Objective

Take the one-sentence culture statement you wrote in July, hold the first half of the year against it, and say honestly how you have done. Then say it to your staff. Most mid-year reflection in schools is done on data about students. This is about whether the thing you said the year would be is actually happening, and whether the person who said it has behaved accordingly.

## Time Needed

25 minutes alone. Then about 10 minutes with staff.

## Supplies / Setup

- The piece of paper from July with your one sentence on it, and your BE words.
- Whatever real evidence you have: attendance, staff survey results, discipline patterns, the unclaimed-student wall photo from August, the Chip Bowl tally, your rounds tracker.
- Your calendar from September through December, which is the most honest document about your priorities that exists.
- Somewhere private, and enough nerve to write down a grade you would not enjoy sharing.

## How It Works

1. **Read the sentence first, before any data (3 minutes).** Read what you wrote in July before you look at anything else, so you are measuring against what you actually said rather than against what you have since decided you meant.
2. **Grade it honestly, with a letter and a reason (7 minutes).** A letter grade forces a position that a paragraph lets you dodge. Then one sentence on why. "B minus, because we did the visible parts and not the hard ones."
3. **Audit your calendar against your sentence (8 minutes).** Go through September to December and mark what you actually spent hours on. Your calendar knows your real priorities better than your intentions do. If your sentence is about staff being known and your calendar is compliance meetings, that is the finding.
4. **Name one thing you personally got wrong (4 minutes).** Not the district, not the budget, not the sub shortage. One thing you did or failed to do. This is the part that makes the whole exercise worth doing and the part every instinct will route you around.
5. **Say all of it to staff (10 minutes with staff, separately).** The sentence, the grade, the calendar finding, the thing you got wrong, and one specific change for the second half. Then stop talking and do not ask them to reciprocate. This is not a reflection exercise for the room; it is you being accountable in front of it.

## Why It Works

- A culture statement nobody revisits is decoration. Grading it in January is the difference between a stated value and an actual one.
- A letter grade cannot be hedged. Every leader can write a paragraph about a mixed year; almost none can write "C plus" without feeling it.
- The calendar audit is brutal and reliable. Intentions live in your head, priorities live in your schedule, and the gap between them is where culture actually gets decided.
- Naming your own error out loud is the single most credibility-generating thing a school leader can do, and it is rare enough that staff remember it for years.
- Doing it in January leaves a full semester to act. The same honesty in May is a post-mortem.

## Script for the Leader

For the staff meeting:

"In July I wrote one sentence about what I wanted it to feel like to work here. Here it is again. Halfway through, I'd give us a C plus, and I want to tell you why. We did the visible things well. The part I said mattered most, that nobody here would be dealing with a hard class alone, I didn't build. I looked at my calendar from September to December and I spent more hours on compliance than in your rooms, and that was my choice, not the district's. So for the second half, Thursday mornings are blocked for classrooms and nothing takes that block. You can hold me to it."

## Facilitator Tips

- Write the grade down before you look for reasons. Reasons found first will negotiate the grade upward.
- One change, not five. A leader announcing five second-half changes in January has announced nothing.
- Make the change structural rather than attitudinal. "I'll be more present" is not a change. "Thursday mornings are blocked" is.
- Do not ask staff to grade themselves in the same meeting. That converts your accountability into an assignment for them and cheapens it.
- Keep the written grade with the July paper. June's closing activity asks for the final grade, and having the January one next to it is the whole story.
- If your honest grade is genuinely an A, say so and give the evidence. False modesty reads as badly as false confidence.
