# Culture Builder: The One Thing We Always Do (September)

Pick one school-wide tradition, launch it in September, and never miss a week.

## Objective

Choose a single thing your whole school does every week, all year, without exception, and start it in September while the year is still soft enough to shape. Not five things. One. A school's identity is built almost entirely out of what it repeats, and most schools repeat nothing on purpose. This is 25 minutes with your staff to pick the one and commit to it in public.

## Time Needed

25 minutes in a staff meeting, in the first three weeks of September.

## Supplies / Setup

- Your one-sentence culture statement, on the screen.
- Chart paper and markers.
- An honest look at your master schedule, so you pick a slot that actually exists.
- Two or three candidate traditions you have already thought about, so the room has somewhere to start.

## How It Works

1. **Name the ones you already have (5 minutes).** Ask staff what this school already does every single week without fail. Write them down. Most schools produce a very short list, and the shortness of it is the argument for doing this. Do not skip this step to save time, because the room needs to feel the gap.
2. **Generate candidates against three tests (7 minutes).** Every idea has to pass all three: it happens weekly, it takes under ten minutes, and it works on your worst week in February with a sub shortage and a broken heater. Ideas that fail the February test get crossed out immediately, and most of the exciting ones do.
3. **Cut to one, out loud (5 minutes).** Vote, or decide yourself and say you are deciding. Both are fine. What is not fine is leaving with three, because three means none. Some that hold up: a Friday all-call that ends the same way every week, a Monday shout-out read over the announcements with real student names, an all-school pause at the same minute every week for something specific.
4. **Fix the day, the time, and the owner (5 minutes).** Write it on the chart paper as a sentence with all four parts: what, which day, what time, who is responsible if that person is absent. A tradition without a named backup dies the first week its owner is out.
5. **Announce it to students and families in week one (3 minutes).** Say what it is, say it happens every week, and say it out loud that you intend to still be doing it in May. Public commitment is the mechanism. Post it where families can read it.

## Why It Works

- Identity is repetition. Students describe their school by what happens over and over, never by what was on a poster in the office.
- One tradition survives. Five compete with each other for the same ten minutes and all five are gone by November, and the collapse teaches staff that nothing here is permanent.
- The February test is the whole filter. It kills the elaborate idea that only works when everything is going well, which is exactly the idea a room full of energized adults in September will vote for.
- Naming a backup owner is the difference between a tradition and a good intention. Most weekly rituals die in the first week the person who runs it is absent.
- Announcing it publicly to students and families raises the cost of quitting, which is precisely what you want.

## Script for the Leader

For the staff meeting:

"I want us to pick one thing this school does every week, all year, no exceptions. One. Not a list. And the test isn't whether it sounds good today, it's whether we'll still be doing it the third week of February when three people are out and the copier's broken. If it doesn't survive that day, it's not a tradition, it's a mood. I'd rather do one small thing 36 weeks in a row than five great things until October."

## Facilitator Tips

- Resist adding a second one, forever. The request will come in January and the answer is no until this one has run a full year.
- Small beats impressive. A ninety-second all-call that never misses builds more identity than a monthly assembly that gets cancelled twice.
- Put it on the master calendar as a recurring block so the tradition has a defended slot rather than borrowed time.
- Track it visibly. A simple wall count of consecutive weeks turns the streak itself into part of the tradition, and staff start protecting the number.
- If you do miss a week, name the miss out loud and restart the count. Pretending it did not happen costs you more than the miss did.
- June, Week 3 asks what to keep and what to kill. This one earns its place by then or it does not, and either answer is useful.

## Display Idea

A wall counter near the main office: "Weeks in a row: 14." Students will notice the number before the adults do, and they will be the ones who tell you if it stalls.
