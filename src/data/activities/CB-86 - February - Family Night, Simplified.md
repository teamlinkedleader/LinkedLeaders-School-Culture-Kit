# Culture Builder: Family Night, Simplified (February)

One room, one hour, one thing to do together, and nothing anyone has to prepare.

## Objective

Plan a family night that a tired family can actually attend and a tired staff can actually run. One hour, one room, one activity families do together, no performance, no presentation, no volunteering, no cost, and no preparation from anyone. Most family nights are built for the families who already come. This is built for the ones who do not, in the month when nobody has energy for anything elaborate.

## Time Needed

25 minutes to plan. One hour to run.

## Supplies / Setup

- One room. The cafeteria or the library, not the gym, because a big empty space with twelve families in it feels like failure.
- One activity that works with any number of people and needs no reading level.
- The attendance list from your last family event, so you can see who did not come.
- Nothing for families to bring. Not food, not a device, not a permission slip.

## How It Works

1. **Cut everything except one hour and one activity (7 minutes).** No presentation from the principal, no curriculum overview, no book fair, no sign-up tables, no student performance. Every one of those exists to serve the school. If you can only keep one thing, keep families sitting at a table doing something together.
2. **Pick an activity that cannot be done wrong (5 minutes).** Board games. A puzzle per table. Building the tallest thing out of spaghetti. Making a family crest on paper. The requirement is that a parent with no English, no education, and no energy can do it alongside their kid and feel competent. Anything with reading or writing at its centre fails that test.
3. **Solve the three real barriers (7 minutes).** Childcare for younger siblings, which is usually the actual reason a family does not come. Food, because 5:30 is dinner and asking a family to eat first is asking them not to come. And transport or timing, which for some of your families is decisive. If you can only fix one, fix childcare by saying explicitly that all ages are welcome in the room.
4. **Invite by phone, not by flyer, for the families you most want (4 minutes).** Pick fifteen families who have never come. A personal call from someone they know, saying "I'd like you to come, there's nothing you need to bring or do," outperforms any number of flyers. Say plainly there is no presentation.
5. **Stay at one table the whole hour (2 minutes to decide).** Decide in advance which table you sit at, and sit there. A principal circulating and greeting is hosting. A principal doing a puzzle with one family for an hour is a person that family knows now.

## Why It Works

- Attendance at family events is not limited by interest, it is limited by barriers. Childcare, food, transport, work schedules, and the fear of being asked to read something aloud.
- A presentation converts a family event into a school event, and it signals that the school's agenda is the point. Removing it is what makes the evening feel like an invitation.
- An activity with no wrong answer is what lets a parent who feels judged by schools have a good hour in one. That hour is what changes the next hard phone call.
- Personal invitations to the specific families you want are the only reliable way to change who comes. The flyer reaches the families who were coming anyway.
- February is exactly right for this. Nobody has energy for a production, and the low-effort version is not a compromise, it is better.

## Script for the Leader

For the phone invitations:

"I'm calling because I'd like you to come Thursday at 5:30. It's an hour, there's food, and honestly there's nothing to it. No presentation, nobody's going to ask you to volunteer, and you don't need to bring anything or prepare anything. We just play board games at the tables. Bring the little ones, that's fine, they're welcome in the room. I'd really like to see you there."

## Facilitator Tips

- Say "all ages welcome in the room" explicitly in every invitation. Childcare is the barrier most often assumed away by staff who have it.
- Feed people something, even if it is toast and fruit donated by a partner. Do not run an evening event with nothing to eat.
- Keep it to one hour and end on time. Families with young children are watching the clock, and an event that overruns will cost you their attendance next time.
- Resist adding a table. Every year somebody wants to add a sign-up, a survey, or a fundraiser, and each addition costs you the families you built it for.
- Track who comes, and specifically who came for the first time. Those names are the outcome, not the headcount.
- Repeat it rather than escalating it. The same modest evening three times a year beats one big production.
