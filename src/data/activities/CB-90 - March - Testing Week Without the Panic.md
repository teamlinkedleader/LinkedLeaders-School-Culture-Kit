# Culture Builder: Testing Week Without the Panic (March)

Write the letter home that lowers the temperature instead of raising it.

## Objective

Send families a letter before testing that reduces anxiety rather than manufacturing it, and agree with staff on what nobody will say to students that week. Schools reliably do the opposite. The pep rallies, the countdown, the "this is important" assemblies and the letter urging a good breakfast all raise the stakes for children who are already frightened, and they raise them most for the students who will do worst.

## Time Needed

20 minutes: about 10 to write the letter and 10 with staff.

## Supplies / Setup

- Last year's letter home about testing, which is likely to be a good example of what not to do.
- The actual testing schedule.
- Your counselor, who knows which students got sick, refused, or fell apart last year.
- A clear head about what the test does and does not decide for a child in your building.

## How It Works

1. **Read last year's letter as an anxious child would (4 minutes).** Underline every phrase that raises stakes. "Very important." "Do your best." "Make sure you get a good night's sleep." That last one, to a ten year old with insomnia, is an instruction to succeed at the thing they cannot do.
2. **Write the honest version (6 minutes).** Say what the test actually is, what it is used for, and what it does not decide. Say the child is not being ranked as a person. Give families two concrete things that genuinely help, and be explicit that pushing their child harder is not one of them. Say that if their child is anxious, they can call you.
3. **Agree what staff will not say (6 minutes).** A short banned list, agreed in the room. No countdowns. No "this shows what you're really made of." No comparing classes or homerooms. No rewards for scores. No mention of consequences for the school, which children absorb as responsibility for adults' jobs.
4. **Agree the two things you will keep normal (2 minutes).** Recess and lunch. Cancelling either during testing week is the most common and most counterproductive decision schools make, and it is usually made for logistics.
5. **Name the students who need a plan and make one (2 minutes).** The refusers, the ones who vomit, the ones with the worst anxiety. A specific plan each, made in advance, that does not involve discovering the problem mid-test.

## Why It Works

- Testing anxiety is heavily transmitted from adults. Children read the building's temperature, and the pep rally that is meant to motivate reads to an anxious child as confirmation that something serious is about to happen to them.
- The stakes-raising messaging harms the lowest performers most, and they are the students the school most needs to keep trying.
- Telling families plainly what the test does not decide is genuinely new information for many of them. Most parents assume it matters more than it does.
- Protecting recess is not softness. Removing physical activity and unstructured time from a week of sustained cognitive demand makes performance worse as well as making children miserable.
- Naming the banned phrases in advance works because these things are said reflexively by well-meaning adults under pressure, not deliberately.

## Script for the Leader

For the letter home:

"Testing starts on the 16th. Here is what it is: a snapshot of some things your child has learned, on one particular week, used mostly by the state and the district to look at schools. Here is what it is not: a measure of how smart your child is, or whether they are doing well here, or anything that changes their classes or their year. Two things that genuinely help: normal bedtime and breakfast. One thing that does not help: telling them it matters. It does not matter that much, and they will do better if nobody in their life is anxious about it. If your child is worried, call me and we will make a plan for them."

## Facilitator Tips

- Do not hold a testing pep rally. If your district requires some form of acknowledgment, keep it factual and brief.
- Protect recess even when the schedule makes it inconvenient. This is the one to fight for.
- Watch your own language in announcements that week. Leaders often ban the phrases and then use them over the intercom.
- Feed the kids. Whatever your breakfast provision is, make it easier that week, and do not make anyone identify themselves to get it.
- Tell staff explicitly that a child refusing to test is not a discipline matter. That is the moment where a well-meaning adult can do real damage.
- Send the same letter, near enough, every year. The consistency is part of what lowers the temperature.
