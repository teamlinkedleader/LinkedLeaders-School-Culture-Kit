# Culture Builder: The Survey People Actually Answer (May)

Three questions, no scale, and a published answer to what people said.

## Objective

Replace the annual forty-question climate survey nobody fills in with three open questions people will actually answer, then publish what came back and what you are changing. Response rates on school surveys are low for a good reason: they are long, they use scales that produce numbers nobody acts on, and families have learned that answering changes nothing.

## Time Needed

20 minutes to write and set up. Then a real half hour later to read and respond.

## Supplies / Setup

- Last year's survey and its response rate, which is the argument for doing this differently.
- Whatever tool you already use, or paper, or a phone call. The channel matters less than the length.
- Translation, sorted in advance.
- A commitment, before you send it, to publish the results even if they are bad.

## How It Works

1. **Cut it to three open questions (5 minutes).** What is one thing this school does well. What is one thing we should change. What is one thing you wish we had asked. No scales, no matrices, no "rate your agreement." Open text is harder to tabulate and enormously more useful.
2. **Say how long it takes and mean it (2 minutes).** "Three questions, about two minutes, and you can answer one and skip the others." An honest time estimate is the single largest driver of completion.
3. **Chase the families you never hear from, by phone (5 minutes to plan).** The families who answer surveys are the ones already engaged. Pick fifteen who never respond and have someone call and ask the three questions out loud. Those fifteen answers will be worth more than the two hundred you collect online.
4. **Read it yourself, all of it (30 minutes later).** Not a summary, not a dashboard. Reading the raw responses is the point, including the angry ones, which are usually the most specific and therefore the most useful.
5. **Publish what you heard and what changes (8 minutes).** A short note: here is what came up most, here is what we are changing, here is what we are not changing and why. The "not changing and why" is the part that makes people answer next year, because it proves the responses were read by a person.

## Why It Works

- Long surveys measure who has time and goodwill, not what the community thinks. Three questions measure something closer to the truth.
- Open text produces actionable specifics. A 3.2 out of 5 on "communication" tells you nothing; "I never know when report cards are coming" tells you exactly what to fix.
- Phoning the families who never respond is the only reliable way to hear from the people whose experience differs most from the ones you already hear from.
- Publishing the results, including the unflattering ones, is what converts a survey into a relationship. Every school that quietly collects feedback and never mentions it again teaches its community not to bother.
- Explaining what you will not change is more credible than a list of promises, and it protects you from the expectation that every suggestion becomes policy.

## Script for the Leader

For the note that publishes the results:

"Two hundred and six of you answered. The thing that came up most was that you find out about things too late, and that was said in some form by about a third of you. So from August, everything goes out on the same day each week, on Thursday, and nothing gets announced with less than a week's notice unless it is an emergency. The second most common was the pickup line, and I am not changing that one, because the alternatives we have looked at make it slower rather than faster. If somebody has a solution we have not thought of, I would genuinely like to hear it."

## Facilitator Tips

- Three questions. The urge to add a fourth is where every good survey dies.
- Do not use a scale anywhere. The moment there is a number, the reporting becomes about the number.
- Sort translation before sending, not after. A survey only available in English measures your English-speaking families.
- Phone calls beat reminders. A second email reminder produces almost nothing; fifteen calls produce your best data.
- Read the angry responses carefully and do not discount them for tone. The most specific feedback usually arrives annoyed.
- Publish within two weeks. A response that arrives in September is not a response.
- Ask the same three questions every year. Comparability comes from consistency, not from length.

## Display Idea

The published response goes in the newsletter and on the entryway wall, with the changes listed plainly and dated. Next May, put the two side by side so people can see what was said and what happened.
