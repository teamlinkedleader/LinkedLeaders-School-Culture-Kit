# Culture Builder: The 10-Minute Rounds

Walk every classroom for 10 minutes each week, not to evaluate, but to be seen.

## Objective

The single most common complaint staff have about their leaders is: "They never come to my room." The 10-Minute Rounds solve this. Once a week, on the same day, you walk every classroom for 10 minutes each. You do not take notes. You do not give feedback. You do not evaluate. You walk in, you watch, you leave. The purpose is not to inspect. The purpose is to be present. Visibility is the culture. A leader who is seen in classrooms every week is a leader who is trusted. A leader who is seen only when there is a problem is a leader who is feared.

## Time Needed

10 minutes per classroom. If you have 30 classrooms, that is 5 hours. Pick one day. Do it every week. Same day, every week.

## Supplies / Setup

- A clipboard or notebook (for your own notes, not for feedback).
- A schedule of the day, with classroom times.
- Comfortable shoes.
- Nothing else.

## How It Works

1. **Pick the day (5 minutes, once).** Choose one day per week and protect it. Tuesday is good — Monday is too chaotic, Friday is too tired. Put it on your calendar as a recurring event. Do not move it. If you move it, you will stop doing it.
2. **Walk in, watch, leave (10 minutes per room).** Enter quietly. Stand or sit in the back. Watch the lesson. Watch the students. Watch the teacher. Do not speak unless spoken to. Do not take notes on the teacher — take notes on what students are doing, what questions they ask, what lights them up. Leave after 10 minutes. Do not say goodbye. Just leave.
3. **Do not give feedback (ongoing).** This is the hardest rule. You will see things you want to fix. Do not fix them. Not this week. The rounds are not coaching. The rounds are presence. If you give feedback, the rounds become evaluation, and the rooms will change when you walk in. You want the rooms to stay the same. That is how you see the real culture.
4. **Track what you see (5 minutes after each round).** After you leave the building, write down three things you noticed: one thing a student did, one thing a teacher did, one thing about the room. Not for feedback. For you. This builds your picture of the school over time. After six weeks, you will know things about your school that no data report can tell you.
5. **Be seen in the halls between rooms (the hidden work).** The 10 minutes in the classroom is the point. But the 2 minutes in the hallway between classrooms is the multiplier. Walk slowly. Talk to the student at their locker. Open the door for the parent with the stroller. Let people see you walking, not rushing. The rounds are not just the classrooms. They are the walking.

## Why It Works

- Presence is trust. A leader who is in classrooms every week is a leader who knows what is happening. A leader who knows what is happening is a leader whose feedback is trusted. A leader whose feedback is trusted is a leader who can lead change.
- Not giving feedback is the mechanism that makes the rounds work. The moment you give feedback, the rounds become evaluation, and the teachers will perform for you. You want to see the real room, not the performance. Silence is the tool.
- Same day, every week. Predictability is the point. If teachers know Tuesday is rounds day, they do not dread it. It becomes part of the week. If the rounds are random, they become a source of anxiety.
- The hallway minutes matter more than the classroom minutes. The classroom is where you learn. The hallway is where you are seen. A leader who is seen walking the halls every Tuesday is a leader who is part of the school, not apart from it.
- After six weeks, you will know your school in a way that no spreadsheet can show you. You will know which teacher is struggling before they tell you. You will know which student is invisible before anyone names it. You will know which room is alive and which room is surviving. That knowledge is the rounds' real product.

## Script for the Leader

For when a teacher asks what you are doing in their room:

"I'm not here to evaluate you. I'm here to be seen. I want to know what is happening in this school, and the only way I can know is to be in the rooms. I'm not taking notes on you. I'm not giving you feedback. I'm just here. If you want to talk about what I saw, come find me after school. But I'm not starting that conversation. You are."

## Facilitator Tips

- Do not announce the rounds. Do not send an email saying "I will be doing classroom walks on Tuesdays." Just start doing them. After three weeks, someone will ask. That is when you explain. Announcing it makes it a program. Just doing it makes it a habit.
- If a teacher is visibly nervous when you enter, that is data. Note it. Do not address it in the moment. After a month of rounds, the nervousness will fade. If it does not fade, that is a conversation — but not a rounds conversation.
- If you see something unsafe, intervene. The "no feedback" rule is for instruction, not for safety. If a student is in danger, you act. But do not confuse "not my preferred teaching style" with "unsafe." They are not the same.
- Do not count the rounds. Do not make a chart of how many rooms you visited. Do not report it to your supervisor as evidence of your visibility. The moment the rounds become a metric, they become a performance. The rounds are a practice, not a report.
- If you miss a week, do not make it up. Do not do two days the next week. Just return to the schedule the following week. Making it up turns it into debt. Debt kills habits. Just return.
- After a semester, you can begin to have conversations. Not feedback — conversations. "I noticed your students always come to the rug quietly. How did you build that?" This is not evaluation. This is curiosity. The rounds give you the raw material for real conversations that do not feel like judgments.
